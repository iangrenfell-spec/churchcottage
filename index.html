I

The Setting

On the Edge of
England

Studland is a village that feels like it exists at the edge of time. Tucked into the eastern tip of the Isle of Purbeck, where chalk downland tumbles into Studland Bay, it is a place shaped equally by geology and by solitude.

To the east, the great chalk headland of Handfast Point breaks apart into the sea stacks of Old Harry Rocks — the easternmost point of the Jurassic Coast UNESCO World Heritage Site, where 80-million-year-old Cretaceous chalk stands sentinel against the Channel. On a clear day, you can see the Needles on the Isle of Wight; the two formations were once joined, before the sea carved the Solent between them.

To the west, Studland Heath stretches inland — a rare lowland heath of European importance, home to all six native British reptile species. To the south, the ridge climbs toward Nine Barrow Down with its Bronze Age burial mounds. The village sits within both the Dorset Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty (now a National Landscape) and the Studland Conservation Area, a designation recognising its exceptional architectural and historic character.

Studland remained remarkably isolated until the twentieth century. There was no ferry across the harbour mouth to Sandbanks until 1926, and no proper road before the Ferry Road was built. The Bankes Estate owned more than 95% of the land and was resistant to development. The result is a village that retains an unusual integrity — a scatter of stone and thatch around a Norman church, largely unchanged in form for centuries.

At the heart of the village stands St Nicholas Church, built in the early twelfth century on the foundations of an earlier Saxon church. Nikolaus Pevsner described it as “one of the most complete Norman village churches in England.” Its squat central tower, three-cell plan of nave, chancel and sanctuary, and beautifully carved Norman arches have survived largely unaltered for nine hundred years. The corbel table beneath the roofline carries grotesque and lascivious carvings — a sheela-na-gig, grinning faces, farm animals — that have watched over the village since the time of Henry I.

It was immediately beside this church, on Manor Road, that Church Cottage stood for three centuries. The two buildings shared more than proximity; they shared a history of ecclesiastical ownership, of chalk and stone, of patient endurance against the Dorset weather.

II

Origins

The Rector’s
Cottage

The documentary record of Church Cottage is, as the archaeologists noted, “not plentiful.” But the building itself told its own story — in its walls, its timbers, and its relationship to the land.

The most compelling theory for the cottage’s origin links it to the glebe lands of the Rector of Studland. In the English parish system, glebe land was an area of land assigned to support the parish priest. The word itself derives from the Latin gleba — a clod of earth, a piece of soil. Glebe could include fields, farms, mills, and houses. A cottage on glebe land beside the parish church would have served as a dwelling for a tenant or worker connected to the ecclesiastical estate.

“It is possible the building was originally erected as a tenanted cottage within the glebe lands of the Rector of Studland.”

— Cotswold Archaeology, Level 4 Historic Building Recording, 2024

The Level 4 Historic Building Recording undertaken by Cotswold Archaeology identified the earliest phase of Church Cottage as a two-cell, asymmetric cottage, likely one-and-a-half storeys in height. It was constructed using local vernacular materials and methods: principally white chalk freestone, the soft Cretaceous limestone that underlies much of the Purbeck landscape.

Chalk freestone is a demanding material. Soft when first quarried, it hardens on exposure to air but remains vulnerable to weathering. It erodes readily when saturated, and so the builders of Church Cottage — like those across the chalk districts of Dorset — typically protected it with a coat of lime render and deep eaves of thatch. The Cotswold Archaeology report identified evidence that the vernacular builders demonstrated a clear “awareness of chalk freestone’s limitations” in their construction methods.

Analysis of the roof trusses during the 2024 watching brief suggested the building may date to “earlier in the 18th century than has previously been thought.” Intriguingly, staggered purlin mortices indicated that the roof may originally have been covered in stone tiles before being re-thatched in reed — a material change that speaks to evolving local building traditions and, perhaps, economics.

The Dorset Heritage Explorer records Church Cottage as an “eighteenth century cottage with plastered cob walls, half-hipped thatched roof, one brick end stack and one near centre.” The Royal Commission on Historical Monuments included it in their 1970 inventory of Dorset’s historical monuments — a measure of its significance even then.

III

Evolution

Three Phases,
Three Centuries

Buildings in Studland do not stand still. Like the chalk cliffs that frame the bay, they are shaped by time, by need, and by the slow accretion of human lives lived within them.

The Cotswold Archaeology team identified three principal phases of construction at Church Cottage. The first — dating to the early eighteenth century or perhaps earlier — was the original two-cell cottage, built of chalk freestone with an inglenook fireplace and bread oven. This was a working building, functional and warm, its thick walls and deep thatch designed to hold heat in winter and shed the driving rain that sweeps in from the Channel.

Around 1823, the building’s story intersected with that of the Bankes family. The Right Honourable George Bankes, whose family seat was at Kingston Lacy near Wimborne Minster, chose Studland as the site for an extraordinary seaside retreat. His “eccentric marine villa” — now known as the Manor House, and today operating as The Pig on the Beach hotel — was a fantasia of gabled dormers, glazed turrets, and small round towers, perhaps inspired by the notion that the site was that of the medieval Studland Castle.

When the Bankes family expanded their presence in Studland, Church Cottage was absorbed into the estate. It joined a landholding that would eventually encompass more than 95% of the village — a concentration of ownership that preserved Studland’s rural character through the centuries of development that transformed much of the Dorset coast.

The second phase of construction, identified as occurring after 1871, saw the cottage extended by one bay to the north. Census records and other evidence suggest the building may have been informally subdivided into as many as three separate dwellings during this period — a common pattern in estate cottages, where growing families and changing agricultural economies drove internal reorganisation.

The distinctive architectural character of Church Cottage was shaped by its materials and setting. The reed-thatched roof with half hips is a signature of the Purbeck vernacular — the half hip being a gentle slope at the gable end where the thatch wraps partway down, rather than terminating at a full gable. This detail is both practical (reducing wind exposure on the exposed Dorset coast) and beautiful, giving the roofline a softness that is characteristic of the region.

Inside, the inglenook fireplace with its bread oven spoke of centuries of domestic life. The beamed ceilings, the thick stone walls, the small-paned windows — all were elements of a building designed for shelter, for warmth, for the quiet rhythms of a rural existence in the lee of a Norman church.

IV

The Twentieth Century

War, Change &
Private Hands

The twentieth century brought upheaval to Studland that the village’s previous centuries of quiet could scarcely have imagined.

In 1981, Ralph Bankes bequeathed the entire estate — Kingston Lacy, Corfe Castle, and all of Studland — to the National Trust. It was one of the largest single bequests in the Trust’s history. But Church Cottage had already left the Bankes estate by then; at some point during the early twentieth century, it had returned to the ownership of the Diocese of Salisbury, the ecclesiastical body that had overseen the glebe lands centuries earlier.

Before the change in ownership reshaped the village, however, Studland played an extraordinary role in the most consequential military operation in modern history.

Studland & D-Day

From autumn 1943, Studland Bay became an amphibious assault training area for the D-Day landings. The beach’s profile closely matched the Normandy coastline, making it ideal for rehearsals.

On 18 April 1944, Winston Churchill, King George VI, Eisenhower, Montgomery, and Mountbatten gathered in the concrete observation bunker at Fort Henry — just along the coast from Church Cottage — to observe Exercise Smash, the largest live ammunition exercise of the entire war. Troops trained with live fire and amphibious DD (Duplex Drive) tanks. Tragically, six men lost their lives when tanks sank during a practice run on 4 April 1944.

To this day, live ammunition from the D-Day rehearsals is still found on Studland Beach.

The Diocese of Salisbury held Church Cottage through much of the twentieth century. Then, in 1996, the cottage passed into private ownership for the first time in its history — no longer part of a glebe, no longer part of an aristocratic estate, no longer held by the Church.

The third and final phase of construction identified by Cotswold Archaeology dates to this period. A further bay was added to the north, replacing an earlier lean-to structure that may have been part of the second phase. This late twentieth-century extension used modern materials and methods, a clear departure from the chalk freestone and vernacular techniques of the original building.

Yet the essential character of Church Cottage endured. It remained a thatched cottage of chalk and stone, sitting in its elevated position beside St Nicholas Church, looking out across Studland Bay to the Isle of Wight. Its Grade II listing, first made in its current form, recognised its significance as part of Studland’s built heritage — a building that contributed to the special character of the Conservation Area and the village’s sense of place.

V

The Collapse

2022

In 2022, the north-west facing elevation of Church Cottage suffered an uncontrolled partial collapse. Three centuries of quiet endurance ended in a sudden failure of the chalk freestone wall.

The collapse was not entirely unexpected in a building of this age and construction. Chalk freestone, for all its local abundance and workability, is inherently vulnerable. When saturated by prolonged rain or groundwater ingress, it softens and loses structural integrity. Over centuries, cycles of wetting and drying, freezing and thawing, can progressively weaken a wall until a critical point is reached.

What followed the collapse was a protracted and sensitive series of discussions involving multiple parties: Dorset Council planners, Conservation Officers, Historic England (the body responsible for the listed building), Building Control, and SPAB — the Society for the Protection of Ancient Buildings, founded by William Morris in 1877 to champion the sympathetic treatment of historic structures.

“Following a collapse of the external wall in 2022, extensive discussions between Dorset Council Planners, Conservation Officers, Historic England, Building Control and SPAB, it was agreed that the building would need to be demolished and a sensitive replacement designed.”

— Western Design Architects

The conclusion, reached after careful structural assessment, was that repair was not economically viable. The building — a Grade II Listed cottage that had stood since the early eighteenth century, that had been home to rector’s tenants, estate workers, and private owners across three hundred years — would need to be demolished.

The loss of any listed building is significant. England’s listed building register exists precisely to protect structures of special architectural or historic interest. To demolish one requires exceptional circumstances. But the collapse of Church Cottage presented exactly those circumstances: a building that could no longer safely stand, whose fabric had reached the end of its structural life.

The decision was made: demolish, but record everything first. And then design a sensitive replacement that would honour what had been lost.

VI

Recording & Remembering

Preserving by
Record

When a historic building cannot be saved, the discipline of archaeology offers a different kind of preservation: preservation by record. The building is lost, but its story is captured in meticulous detail for future generations.

Cotswold Archaeology, one of England’s leading archaeological practices, was commissioned to undertake a Level 4 Historic Building Recording — the most comprehensive level of building recording defined by Historic England. Level 4 provides an analytical record that seeks to understand the building’s development, construction techniques, and significance in full detail.

The recording was conducted in two stages. Stage 1, carried out in 2023, documented the standing building before demolition — its structure, materials, spatial organisation, and architectural details. Stage 2 comprised a watching brief during the demolition itself in January 2024, allowing archaeologists to examine structural elements that were only revealed as the building came apart.

Roof Trusses

Historic roof trusses were extracted and recorded analytically and photographically during demolition. Their form suggested dating “earlier in the 18th century than has previously been thought.”

Purlin Mortices

Staggered purlin mortices indicated the building may once have had stone tile roofing — a heavier covering that preceded the reed thatch, speaking to changes in local building tradition.

Wall Construction

The vernacular builders’ awareness of chalk freestone’s limitations was evident in their construction methods — thicker walls, protective render, and careful selection of stone.

Building Phases

Three clear phases of construction were identified, from the original 18th-century two-cell cottage through northward expansion to the late 20th-century extension.

The completed report — authored by James Barnes and Sacha Hunter of Cotswold Archaeology (Ref: CR1246, published May 2024) — now resides in the Dorset Heritage Explorer as source SDO21131, and in the national Archaeology Data Service. It is the definitive record of a building that no longer exists; a detailed account of construction techniques, materials, and spatial organisation that contributes to the wider understanding of vernacular building in the Purbeck chalk districts.

In the language of heritage management, Church Cottage has been “preserved by record.” The building is gone, but its story is not.

VII

Rebirth

A Sensitive
Replacement

Western Design Architects were appointed to design a replacement for Church Cottage — a task that demanded sensitivity, imagination, and a deep understanding of what the original building meant to its setting.

The design philosophy was clear from the outset: the new cottage would echo the original in form, mass, and height, actively preserving the street scene and respecting the special character of the Studland Conservation Area. The late twentieth-century northern extension — Phase Three in the archaeological record — was removed, allowing the replacement to better represent the original historic structure.

The new cottage occupies the existing footprint and retains the existing building line. The hipped gable ends, chimney location, and front porch have all been retained to replicate the existing arrangement. The principal materials — lime render and reed thatch — continue the local building tradition. Timber sash windows maintain the existing proportions with improved alignment.

Where the design departs from the original is at the rear, with a single-storey contemporary extension. This is deliberately different in character: timber-clad with a sedum (green) roof, it creates a clear delineation between the traditional cottage form and the new. The materials complement the setting, drawing inspiration from the coastal and natural landscape — the timber echoing the driftwood palette of the beach, the sedum a nod to the heathland that borders the village.

Ground Source Heat Pump

Three boreholes draw stable warmth from the earth beneath — the same Purbeck geology that provided the original cottage’s chalk freestone.

20 Solar Panels

A photovoltaic array generates clean energy, discretely integrated into the design alongside the traditional thatch.

Triple Glazing

High-performance windows ensure thermal efficiency while maintaining the proportions and character of the original fenestration.

Salvaged Stone

Chalk freestone salvaged from the demolition of the original cottage is reused in new terracing walls — the old building lives on in the landscape.

Western Design Architects noted that the planning process, despite the sensitivity of the project, was “straightforward” — a testament to the collaborative approach between all parties. The new building will be inspected on completion and delisted, its Grade II designation no longer applying to a structure that is newly built. But the architects expressed confidence that “the cottage will live on in its new form for years to come.”

It is a careful, considered piece of architecture: one that acknowledges loss while looking forward, that uses traditional materials alongside contemporary technology, and that ensures this particular spot on Manor Road — beside a Norman church, overlooking the bay — continues to be occupied by a building that belongs there.
